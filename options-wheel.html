Strategy map

The options wheel on RY

Sell a put. If it expires, sell another. If you get assigned, sell calls against the shares until they're called away — then start over. Every branch below just tells you which of two positions you land in.

The four branches

Each expiry resolves one of these. Nothing else can happen.

Put expires

RY finishes above your put strike

Nobody makes you buy shares at a price above the market. The contract dies, the premium is yours free and clear, and the cash you set aside is released.

Sell another put — nothing changed
Put assigned

RY finishes below your put strike

You buy 100 shares at the strike, whatever RY is actually trading at. Because you already banked the premium, your real cost basis is the strike minus that premium.

Move to selling covered calls
Call expires

RY finishes below your call strike

Nobody takes the shares at a price above the market. You keep the premium and you keep the stock — plus any dividend that paid while you held it.

Sell another call on the same shares
Called away

RY finishes above your call strike

The shares get bought from you at the call strike. You bank the gap between the two strikes on top of every premium you collected on the way round.

Full turn done — back to step 1

Your cost basis after assignment was $172.90 — the $175 strike less the $2.10 you already collected. That's the price RY has to stay above for the position to be in the black.

What to watch

The wheel is steady, not safe. Three things bite.

RY can keep falling

Assignment isn't the risk — being stuck under your basis is. If RY drops well below $172.90, every call strike worth selling sits below what you paid, so you can't get called away without booking a loss.

Your upside is capped

If RY gaps to $200, you still hand the shares over at $182. The wheel deliberately trades open-ended upside for a steadier, smaller stream of premiums.

It ties up real cash

One contract is 100 shares, so a single RY put needs roughly $17,000–18,000 sitting in the account to be genuinely cash-secured. That capital is committed for the whole turn.

Rolling — buying back an option and reselling it further out — is the common escape hatch on either leg, and it changes the arrows above. This page shows the plain wheel with no rolling. Estimates only, not financial advice.
